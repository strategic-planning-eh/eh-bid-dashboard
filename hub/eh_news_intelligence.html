The Big Picture

What it all adds up to — in six short reads

Six weeks of stand-off. Two peace attempts collapsed, the Strait of Hormuz is carrying about a tenth of its normal traffic, and the fighting has turned the Red Sea — the Kingdom's back-up export route and EH's home coast — into an active front line: the Jazan refinery hit twice, the Abqaiq plant struck, tankers burning within sight of Yanbu. The half-year company results show a split economy: Aramco sold oil at $108 and made record profits while SABIC swung to a loss. And two answers arrived: Saudi Arabia is choosing to keep its customers rather than profit from the crisis, and the government's budget is being propped up by the very war that shut its factories.

No quick end in sight

The peace effort collapsed twice in six weeks — most recently in mid-August, when the president declared no talks were happening at all. The US Navy keeps blockading Iranian ports, and only about 73 ships passed through Hormuz in a whole week (before the war, that many passed in a single day). The clearest signal comes from companies, not politicians: Abu Dhabi's oil firm spent $1.3 billion buying its own supertankers, and Aramco is asking customers to plan for back-up ports. Business has stopped waiting for peace and started building around a long war. The oil price agrees — it fell to $80 when a deal looked close, then climbed back above $90 when it fell apart.

The Red Sea is now our front yard — literally

Almost every warning sign we set for Yanbu has come true, short of a direct hit on the port itself: tankers struck off the coast on 5 and 24 August, the Jazan refinery suspended and set on fire again, a declared blockade of Saudi-linked ships, and less oil visibly loading at Yanbu. Riyadh's answer — a mutual-defence pact with Turkey and Pakistan — says it expects a long fight. For EH the message is simple: oil-spill response, water monitoring around Yanbu and Jazan, and emergency planning are no longer future opportunities. They are needs that exist today, on our own coastline.

Saudi Arabia chose customers over war profits

We asked last issue: would September's official oil price for Asia swing to a premium (cashing in on the crisis) or stay discounted (protecting customers)? Answer: discounted even deeper — $2 below the regional average, the cheapest in six years — even though Aramco was actually selling at $108. The Kingdom is protecting its customer base for the long term. The cost shows up at home: Aramco's huge dividend was only about half covered by spare cash last quarter, and it borrowed a little more to pay it in full. That dividend is the state's main artery, and right now it runs on war prices. October's price and next quarter's cash are the test.

The budget's strange balancing act

The deficit shrank dramatically in Q2 — because war-driven oil prices lifted the government's oil income by 22%. But six months in, about 97% of the whole year's planned deficit is already spent, debt keeps climbing, and spending keeps rising. In plain terms: expensive oil is papering over the cost of the war. If peace brings prices down before the factories and exports recover, the hole could grow to roughly double the plan — the scenario the big banks are modelling. Either way, money stays tight: the environmental work that gets funded will be the must-do kind — safety, compliance, water security — not optional studies.

The shut factories are building up a mountain of restart work

SABIC's results put numbers on the shutdown — a third of sales volumes gone, a quarterly loss, and its formal shutdown notice now in a sixth month with no return date. Sadara's silence is ten weeks past its loan deadline with no announcement in either direction. Every extra week adds to the eventual restart job: renewing permits, restarting wastewater systems, safety and environmental checks across five SABIC lines and all 26 Sadara units. That demand is coiling up like a spring — but the release date will be decided in peace talks, not in Jubail. Meanwhile, work continues where the war allows: the giant AI data-centre at NEOM's Oxagon port starts construction within months, and Acwa — despite a 36% profit dip caused mostly by paperwork delays — won the exclusive national right to export green fuels.

    ٤ · لغز التسعير. سألنا في العدد الماضي: هل يتحوّل سعر آسيا الرسمي لسبتمبر إلى علاوة (جنيًا للأزمة) أم يبقى مخصومًا (حمايةً للعملاء)؟ الجواب: خصمٌ أعمق — دولاران دون متوسط المنطقة، الأرخص منذ ست سنوات — رغم أن أرامكو كانت تبيع فعليًا عند ~108$.
    ٥ · المالية العامة. تقلّص العجز درامانيًا في الربع الثاني — لأن أسعار حرب النفط رفعت الإيراد النفطي 22%. لكن بعد ستة أشهر، استُهلك نحو 97% من عجز العام المخطط، والدين يتصاعد، والإنفاق يرتفع: نفطٌ غالٍ يُغطّي مؤقتًا كلفة الحرب.
    ٦ · سابك وصدارة. نتائج سابك وضعت أرقامًا على التوقف — ثلث الأحجام ذهب، وخسارة فصلية، وإشعار الإغلاق الرسمي في شهره السادس بلا موعد عودة. وصمتُ صدارة تجاوز عشرة أسابيع بعد استحقاق القرض بلا إعلان في أي اتجاه — وكل أسبوع إضافي يرفع الفاتورة النهائية.

    المنهجية وتغطية التحقق

    تُزاوج هذه الدورة بين مسحٍ موثّق من المصادر للربع الثاني وطبقة تحقّق صريحة: كل بند يحمل وسمه (مؤكد / مؤكد ومتجاوَز / غير مؤكد)، مع محفّزات تصعيد والدورة القادمة ~مطلع سبتمبر. قائمة الجهات الموثّقة هذه الدورة في النسخة الإنجليزية.

    Environmental Horizons — الاستخبارات الاستراتيجية. أُعدّ للاستخدام الداخلي (الاستراتيجية، وفرز الشراكات، وأولويات الاستثمار) وليس للتوزيع الخارجي. المحتوى تحليلي معلوماتي وليس مشورة استثمارية أو قانونية أو مالية. التواريخ بصيغة يوم/شهر/سنة. اكتمل التعريب: جميع القصص الأربع والعشرين مترجمة بنصّها الكامل أعلاه؛ وتبقى البطاقات التفاعلية والرسوم في النسخة الإنجليزية مرجعًا للأرقام.
