The Big Picture

What it all adds up to — in eight short reads, including what to watch next

Ten weeks on, the war has stopped being a windfall and become a cost. The Ministry of Finance's July–September report shows oil income down 23% because the Kingdom could not ship enough — the gap for the first nine months already equals the whole year-end estimate. Yet corporate Saudi Arabia is recovering faster than the state: Aramco's cash covers its dividend again, Petro Rabigh and SABIC are back in profit, Acwa's profit jumped 71%. The front line moved to EH's own coast and corridor — the East–West pipeline, Yanbu, Rabigh, even Aramco's headquarters — and the regulator turned the waste opportunity into enforceable, biddable demand: 6,449 violations in a quarter, SAR 5 million marine fines, and MWAN's first priced slate of 27 facilities.

The war is now a cost, not a windfall

April–June's smaller gap was a one-off. In July–September oil income fell 23% while Brent averaged about $91, because the barrels were missing; the nine-month gap is SAR 244 billion, 148% of the plan, and 2027 is budgeted with a SAR 191 billion gap financed by borrowing. Spending did fall 4% in the quarter — the first restraint of the war — so government buyers will fund compliance and must-do infrastructure, not optional studies. The full arithmetic is on the hub's Government Finances page.

Companies are recovering faster than the state

Aramco earned $31.2 billion and its spare cash covered the dividend; Petro Rabigh made SAR 2.6 billion in a quarter; SABIC swung to profit; Acwa's profit rose 71% — thanks to refining margins and rerouted logistics. EH's industrial clients have money and growing maintenance budgets; its government clients are stretched. Marafiq's 41% profit fall is the reminder that Jubail still runs below normal.

The front line moved to our coast

Drones from Iraq shut the East–West pipeline for two weeks; the Houthis took the islands at the Red Sea's southern gate, then lost the Mokha coast to government forces; missiles reached Aramco's Dhahran headquarters; Yanbu, Rabigh, Ras Tanura and Abqaiq were claimed targets, and civil-defence alerts reached Jeddah. Spill response, remediation and emergency planning are now a live, local need — on the Gulf coast as well as the Red Sea.

Hormuz is open but dangerous; Bab el-Mandeb is contested

Oil flows are near pre-war levels through escorted corridors — October was the strongest export month since February — but Iran keeps the strait formally closed, October saw the most ship attacks of the war, and the US struck Iranian air defences on 6–7 November while talks continued. Plan for a long period of elevated maritime risk with gradual Gulf-coast restarts.

Regulation is doing the market's work

NCEC's 6,449 quarterly violations and SAR 33 million in fines, marine-pollution fines of up to SAR 5 million with mandatory spill reporting, Riyadh's dust enforcement, 17,000 permits issued this year, and MWAN's priced SAR 2.1 billion slate of 33 facilities turn the "$112 billion opportunity" into enforceable, biddable demand — the strongest structural tailwind for EH's permitting, monitoring, medical-waste and treatment services. The new procurement law (direct purchases up to SAR 1 million, four-day Ministry review, pay existing suppliers first) changes how that demand is bought from around the turn of the year.

PIF's strategy is reshaping who EH competes with — and partners with

Waste and recycling (SIRC) now sit in a named PIF ecosystem beside Acwa, and PIF wants private operators to build. At the same time Veolia signed its way into Acwa, Ma'aden and GASCO's storage arm in a single day, and HUMAIN's data-centre build-out has Aramco's balance sheet behind it. EH's route to scale is as a regional partner and specialist subcontractor on these platforms — in Makkah, Madinah and the Red Sea corridor first.
